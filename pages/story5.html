Alex Fernando: Licensing Music Ethically

A composer’s approach to fair rights, transparent usage and sustainable collaboration between creators and indie studios.

Alex Fernando composes for games, but he treats licensing as part of the creative workflow rather than a legal afterthought. For Alex, ethical licensing is not about adding extra rules, it's about removing ambiguity. If the usage terms are unclear, the collaboration becomes fragile: teams hesitate to implement dynamic audio, creators lose control of how work is reused and projects accumulate hidden risk.

“Indie development moves fast” Alex explains. “A track might start as menu music, then become a looped exploration layer, then split into stems for adaptive mixing. If the agreement doesn’t match what games actually do with audio, it breaks down.”

Alex’s priority is clarity. He wants developers to understand exactly what they are allowed to do (and what they are not), while creators keep visibility over where their work can travel; across updates, ports, marketing material and future releases.

Ethical licensing, in practice, means reducing the chance of conflict later. It protects creative labour while keeping production moving.

“The easiest way to exploit creative work is to keep the terms vague; ethics starts with clarity”

What “Ethical” Looks Like in Practice

For Alex, ethical licensing starts with practical questions: Can the developer edit the track? Can they split stems? Can the music be used in trailers or marketing? Does the license cover a port to console? Is usage limited to one project, or does it extend to sequels?

When these questions are avoided, creators often lose leverage and studios absorb uncertainty. Both outcomes damage trust. Alex prefers licensing structures that make permissions explicit and predictable, so teams can design interactive audio with confidence.

Clear terms don’t restrict creativity but enable it. When a studio knows what is permitted, audio can be implemented more dynamically instead of being reduced to “safe” static loops.

Why Platforms Matter

Alex believes platforms can make ethical licensing easier or push the burden back onto people. Most generic music platforms treat audio as a single file and a single transaction. Game audio rarely works that way. It has versions, variations and implementation notes that change how it is used.

“If a platform doesn’t understand interactive use, it forces creators to rewrite terms each time and developers end up guessing what’s safe” Alex says. “That’s where problems start.”

Better infrastructure means licensing is built into the workflow: clear usage categories, default permissions that reflect real engine behaviour and tools that support layered delivery rather than one track assumptions.

For Alex, licensing is not separate from creativity. It is how creative labour is respected. Ethical systems don’t slow teams down; they reduce uncertainty and make collaboration safer for everyone. When terms match real game audio workflows, developers can build more responsive sound and creators can keep ownership and trust intact.
