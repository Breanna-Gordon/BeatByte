Suki Lee: Learning Game Audio Without an Engineering Background

A creator’s journey into interactive audio and building technical confidence through creative practice rather than formal engineering training.

Suki didn’t start her career in game development, or even in technology. With a background in composition and visual arts, her early work focused on experimental music and installation pieces. Interactive audio felt exciting, but also intimidating.

“I always assumed game audio required a deep engineering background” she says. “I understood storytelling through sound, but I didn’t know how that translated into systems and implementation.”

That gap between creative skill and technical confidence is one many creators experience when first approaching interactive media.

“I realised I didn’t need to become an engineer. I needed tools and workflows that made the technical side feel like an extension of my creative process.”

Bridging Creative and Technical Thinking

Suki began learning game audio by focusing on structure rather than code. Loop points, layers and transitions became creative building blocks and not engineering problems. By thinking in terms of interaction and variation, she gradually developed an understanding of how music behaves inside a game system.

Instead of writing long linear tracks, she experimented with modular phrases that could adapt to gameplay states. This shift in mindset helped her see technical preparation as part of composition, rather than a separate discipline.

Learning Through Practice

Hands on experimentation played a bigger role than formal tutorials. By testing how audio behaved in interactive contexts, Suki built confidence step by step. Mistakes became part of the learning process rather than signs she “wasn’t technical enough.”

That's when she discovered BeatByte's Creator Tools. Recommended by a fellow composer, the platform offered exactly what she'd been searching for. It provided guidance tailored specifically for preparing music for game engines. Instead of navigating generic tutorials or decoding technical documentation alone, she found structured workflows that explained loop points, layering techniques and export formats in language that made sense to her as a musician. "It finally felt like someone understood what composers actually need" she says. "I could focus on the creative decisions while the tools handled the technical formatting."

For Suki, entering game audio wasn’t about replacing creativity with technical expertise, it was about expanding her creative practice into interactive space. With the right support and tools, creators from non-engineering backgrounds can contribute meaningfully to game development without losing their artistic voice.
